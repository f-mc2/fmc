2 Derivative and differentiability

NoteDerivative and differentiability

Definition 1 Consider the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) and a accumulation point \(x_{0}\in D\).

For \(c>0\), define the function \(\Delta f\colon (x_{0}-c,x_{0}+c)\subset D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) given by \[ \Delta f(h):=\frac{f(x_{0}+h) - f(x_{0})}{h} . \] If the limit \[ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\;\Delta f = l \] exists with \(l\in\mathbb{R}\), we say that \(l\) is the derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\), and that \(f\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\).

When \(f\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\), we write \[ f'(x_{0})\equiv \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\;\Delta f. \] We also write \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d} x}(x_{0})\equiv f'(x_{0}), \] and refer to this expression as the Leibniz notation for derivatives.

If \(f\) is differentiable at all \(x\in D\), we say that \(f\) is differentiable in \(D\).

The function determined by the rule \(x\mapsto f'(x)\) (whenever it makes sense) is called the derivative of \(f\). Its domain need not coincide with the domain of \(f\).

Before we learn some cool tricks that make computing (a lot but not all) derivatives a sort of mechanical task that does not require computing limits, let’s work out some derivatives from the very definition.

TipThree simple examples

Example 1 The first example of derivative we consider is that of a constant function \(f(x)=c\) with \(c\in \mathbb{R}\). Following the very definition of derivative given above, we have \[ f'(x_{0})=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}=0 \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

The second example we consider is that of the function \(f(x)=x\): \[ f'(x_{0})=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{x_{0} + h -x_{0}}{h}=1 \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

The third example we consider is that of the function \(f(x)=x^{2}\): \[ \begin{split} f'(x_{0})&=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}= \\ &=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{(x_{0} + h)^{2} -x_{0}^{2}}{h}= \\ &\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{2x_{0}h + h^{2}}{h}=2x_{0} \end{split} \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

We defined the derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\) assuming \(x_{0}\) is an accumulation point. However, we can also relax this assumption to the case where \(x_{0}\) is either a left or right accumulation point, obtaining the notions of left/right derivative and differentiability.

CautionRight and left derivative

Exercise 1 Consider the function \(f\colon D\subseteq \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\).

If \(x_{0}\in D\) is a left or right accumulation point, define the right or left derivative and differentiability of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\) using left/right limits, and prove that, when \(x_{0}\) is both a left and right accumulation point, the differentiability of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\) is equivalent to left and right differentiability.

Solution: You are strongly invited to try to solve it on your own.

Just as it happens for limits, derivatives behave well with respect to restrictions.

CautionDerivative of the restriction

Exercise 2 Consider \(D,D'\subseteq \mathbb{R}\) such that \(D\cap D'\neq \emptyset\), and consider two functions \(f\colon D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) and \(g\colon D' \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) such that \(g(x)=f(x)\) on \(S\subseteq D\cap D'\), and such that both are differentiable at \(x_{0}\in S\). Prove that \[ f'(x_{0})=g'(x_{0}), \] and prove analogous results for the case of left and right derivatives.

Solution: You are strongly invited to try to solve it on your own.

Once a definition is in place, it’s always a good idea to go searching for situations in which the definition itself can not be applied.

Let us consider the absolute value function \(|\cdot|\colon \mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \[ |x|=\sqrt{x^{2}}=\left\{\begin{matrix}x & \mbox{ if } x\geq 0 \\ & \\ -x & \mbox{ if } x<0 .\end{matrix}\right. \] For all points \(x_{0}\neq 0\), we can compute the derivative following the second example above (essentially because of Exercise 2). However, when \(x_{0}=0\), we have to take into account the “sudden change” of the function. Therefore, we first compute the right and left derivatives: \[ \begin{split} \lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{x-0}{x}&=1 \\ & \\ \lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{-}}\frac{-x-0}{x}&=-1 . \end{split} \] Since the two derivatives exist but are different, we conclude that \(|x|\) is not differentiable at \(0\) (but it is separately left and right differentiable there).

3 Properties of derivatives

The first “property” of derivatives we want to highlight is that it requires continuity.

NoteDifferentiability implies continuity

Proposition 1 If the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is (left/right) differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D\), then it is also (left/right) continuous at \(x_{0}\).

Proof: You are strongly invited to try to prove it on your own.

Note, however, that the converse of the previous proposition does not hold. An explicit counterexample is given by the absolute value function we discussed before

Next, we pass to how derivatives behave with respect to sums and products of 2 functions.

NoteAlgebraic manipulations of derivatives

Proposition 2 Given \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\) and the functions \(f,g\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D\), the functions \(h,r\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \(h:=\alpha f+\beta g\) and \(r:=f\cdot g\) are differentiable at \(x\in D\) and their derivatives are \[ \begin{split} h'(x)&=\alpha f'(x) + \beta g'(x),\\ r'(x)& =f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x). \end{split} \] The formula for the derivative of the product of two functions is usually referred to as the Leibniz rule.

What happens if we have more than 2 functions? Luckily, it turns out we can safely generalize Proposition 2. To better digest the generalization, let’s consider an instructive example concerning the product of \(n\) functions.

CautionDerivative of \(x^{n}\)

Exercise 3 Consider the “raise to the power \(n\)” function \((\cdot)^{n}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\). Prove that \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n})=nx^{n-1}. \]

Solution: Clearly, we can decompose \(x^{n}\) into the product of two functions, say \(f(x)=x\) and \(g(x)=x^{n-1}\), and apply the Leibniz Rule: \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n})=x^{n-1} + x \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-1}). \] Now, we can decompose \(x^{n-1}\) into the product of two functions, say \(f(x)=x\) and \(g(x) = x^{n-2}\), and apply the Leibniz Rule: \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-1})=x^{n-2} + x \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-3}), \] which means \[ \begin{split} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n})&=x^{n-1} + x \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-1})= \\ &=x^{n-1} + x\left(x^{n-2} + x \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-3})\right)= \\ &=2x^{n-1} + x^{2} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} x}(x^{n-3}). \end{split} \] We can keep going until we hit \(x^{n-(n-1)}=x\) \[ ff \]

What happens when we have more than 2 functions? For a small number of functions, say between 3 and 7, it should be not extremely boring to use the associativity property of function sums and products to understant how to generalize Proposition 2. For an arbitrary finite number of functions, the generalization can be obtained using the induction principle.

CautionAlgebraic manipulations of derivatives continued

Exercise 4 Let \((\alpha_{1},\cdots\alpha_{n})\) be a finite sequence of real numbers and \((f_{1},\cdots,f_{n})\) a finite sequence of scalar functions with common domain \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). Suppose that, at \(x_{0}\in D\), the derivatives \(f_{j}'(x_{0})\) exist for every \(j=1,...,n\). Then, it holds \[ \begin{split} \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) &=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\, \alpha_{j}\,f_{j}'(x_{0}) \\ \left(\prod_{j=1}^{n} \,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) &=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}'(x_{0})\,\left(\prod_{k\neq j}\alpha_{k}\,f_{k}(x_{0})\right). \end{split} \]

Solution (by induction on \(n\)):

Fix an accumulation point \(x_0\in D\) and assume that \(f_j'(x_0)\) exists for all \(j=1,\dots,n\). Set \(g_j:=\alpha_j f_j\), so that \(g_j'(x_0)=\alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\).

We will use the two-function rules from Proposition 2

We start with the derivative of the sum of finitely many functions.

For \(h\neq 0\) with \(x_0+h\in D\), we have \[ \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j(x_0+h)-\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j(x_0)}{h} =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j \frac{f_j(x_0+h)-f_j(x_0)}{h}. \] Following Definition 1, we take the limit as \(h\to 0\): \[ \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_j(x_0+h)-f_j(x_0)}{h} =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j'(x_0), \] where we passed the limit through the sum because it’s a sum of finitely many elements.

Now, we pass to the derivative of the product of finitely many functions.

Define \[ P_n(x):=\prod_{j=1}^n g_j(x). \]

Base case (\(n=1\)): \(P_1=g_1\), hence \[ P_1'(x_0)=g_1'(x_0) =\sum_{j=1}^1 g_j'(x_0)\prod_{k\neq j} g_k(x_0), \] so the formula holds.

Inductive step: assume that for some \(n\ge 1\), \[ P_n'(x_0)=\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^n g_k(x_0). \] Now \(P_{n+1}=P_n\,g_{n+1}\). By the two-function product rule, \[ P_{n+1}'(x_0)=P_n'(x_0)\,g_{n+1}(x_0)+P_n(x_0)\,g_{n+1}'(x_0). \] Substitute the inductive hypothesis into the first term: \[ \begin{aligned} P_{n+1}'(x_0) &=\left(\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^n g_k(x_0)\right) g_{n+1}(x_0) + \left(\prod_{k=1}^n g_k(x_0)\right) g_{n+1}'(x_0)\\[4pt] &=\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^{n+1} g_k(x_0) \;+\; g_{n+1}'(x_0)\prod_{k=1}^n g_k(x_0)\\[4pt] &=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1} g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^{n+1} g_k(x_0). \end{aligned} \] Finally, replacing \(g_j=\alpha_j f_j\) and \(g_j'(x_0)=\alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\), we obtain \[ \left(\prod_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j\right)'(x_0) =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\left(\prod_{k\neq j}\alpha_k f_k(x_0)\right). \]

This concludes the proof.

Now, we consider what happens to the derivative of the quotient of two functions.

Finally, we are able to use all the results presented in this section to compute the derivatives of polynomial and rational functions without ever computing a limit.

TipDerivatives of polynomials and rational functions

Example 2 Let \(P_{n},Q_{m}\colon \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be polynomial functions. We now compute \(P_{n}',Q_{m}'\) and \(R'\) with \(R=\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{m}}\).

To compute \(P_{n}'\) we first write \[ P_{n}'(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}\,x^{k} \] with \(\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\), and then invoke Exercise 4 to conclude that \(P_{n}'\) is the polynomial of order \((n-1)\) given by \[ P_{n}'(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\alpha_{k}\,x^{k-1}. \] A similar result holds for \(Q_{m}'(x)\).
