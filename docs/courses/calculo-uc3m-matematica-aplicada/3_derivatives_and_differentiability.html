2 Derivative and differentiability

Let’s start with the very definition of derivative at a point.

NoteDerivative and differentiability

Definition 1 Consider the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) and a accumulation point \(x_{0}\in D\).

Define the set of admissible increments \[ H_{x_{0}}:=\{h\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\,:\,x_{0}+h\in D\}, \] and the function \(\Delta f\colon H_{x_{0}}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) by \[ \Delta f(h):=\frac{f(x_{0}+h) - f(x_{0})}{h} . \] Note that \(h=0\) is an accumulation point of \(H_{x_{0}}\).

If the limit \[ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\Delta f(h)=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}= l \] exists with \(l\in\mathbb{R}\), we say that \(l\) is the derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\), that \(f\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\), and we write \[ f'(x_{0})\equiv \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\Delta f(h)=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}=l. \] We also write \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d} x}(x_{0})\equiv f'(x_{0}), \] and refer to this expression as the Leibniz notation for derivatives.

If \(f\) is differentiable at all \(x\in D\), we say that \(f\) is differentiable in \(D\).

The function determined by the rule \(x\mapsto f'(x)\) (whenever it makes sense) is called the derivative of \(f\). Its domain need not coincide with the domain of \(f\).

Before we learn some useful tricks that make computing (a lot but not all) derivatives a sort of mechanical task that does not require computing limits, let’s work out some derivatives from the very definition.

TipDerivative of constant functions

Example 1 The first example of derivative we consider is that of a constant function \(f(x)=c\) with \(c\in \mathbb{R}\). Following the very definition of derivative given above, we have \[ f'(x_{0})=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}=0 \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

TipDerivative of linear functions

Example 2 The second example we consider is that of the linear function \(f(x)=ax\) with \(a\in\mathbb{R}\): \[ f'(x_{0})=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{a(x_{0} + h) -ax_{0}}{h}=a \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

TipDerivative of quadratic functions

Example 3 The third example we consider is that of the function \(f(x)=ax^{2}\): \[ \begin{split} f'(x_{0})&=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{f(x_{0}+h)-f(x_{0})}{h}= \\ &=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{a(x_{0} + h)^{2} -ax_{0}^{2}}{h}= \\ &\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \frac{2ax_{0}h + ah^{2}}{h}=2ax_{0} \end{split} \] for every \(x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\).

We defined the derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\) assuming \(x_{0}\) is an accumulation point. However, we can also relax this assumption to the case where \(x_{0}\) is either a left or right accumulation point, obtaining the notions of left/right derivative and differentiability.

CautionRight and left derivative

Exercise 1 Consider the function \(f\colon D\subseteq \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\).

Solution: You are strongly invited to try to solve it on your own.

Just as it happens for limits, derivatives behave well with respect to restrictions.

Once a definition is in place, it’s always a good idea to go searching for situations in which the definition itself can not be applied.

TipAbsolute value function is not differentiable at \(x=0\)

Example 4 Let us consider the absolute value function \(|\cdot|\colon \mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \[ |x|=\sqrt{x^{2}}=\left\{\begin{matrix}x & \mbox{ if } x\geq 0 \\ & \\ -x & \mbox{ if } x<0 .\end{matrix}\right. \] For all points \(x_{0}\neq 0\), we can compute the derivative following Example 2 (essentially because of Exercise 2). However, when \(x_{0}=0\), we have to take into account the “sudden change” of the function. Therefore, we first compute the right and left derivatives: \[ \begin{split} \lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{x-0}{x}&=1 \\ & \\ \lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{-}}\frac{-x-0}{x}&=-1 . \end{split} \] Since the two derivatives exist but are different, we conclude that \(|x|\) is not differentiable at \(0\) (but it is separately left and right differentiable there).

3 Properties of derivatives

The first “property” of derivatives we want to highlight is that it requires continuity.

NoteDifferentiability implies continuity

Proposition 1 If the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is (left/right) differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D\), then it is also (left/right) continuous at \(x_{0}\).

Proof: You are strongly invited to try to prove it on your own.

Note, however, that the converse of the previous proposition does not hold. An explicit counterexample is given by the absolute value function in Example 4.

Next, we pass to how derivatives behave with respect to sums and products of 2 functions.

NoteAlgebraic manipulations of derivatives

Proposition 2 Given \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\) and the functions \(f,g\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D\), the functions \(h,r\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \(h:=\alpha f+\beta g\) and \(r:=f\cdot g\) are differentiable at \(x_{0}\) and their derivatives are \[ \begin{split} h'(x_{0})&=\alpha f'(x_{0}) + \beta g'(x_{0}),\\ r'(x_{0})& =f'(x_{0})\,g(x_{0}) + f(x_{0})\,g'(x_{0}). \end{split} \] The formula for the derivative of the product of two functions is usually referred to as the Leibniz rule.

What happens when we have more than 2 functions? For a small number of functions, say between 3 and 7, it should be not extremely boring to use the associativity property of function sums and products to understand how to generalize Proposition 2. For an arbitrary finite number of functions, the generalization can be obtained using the induction principle.

CautionAlgebraic manipulations of derivatives continued

Exercise 3 Let \((\alpha_{1},\cdots\alpha_{n})\) be a finite sequence of real numbers and \((f_{1},\cdots,f_{n})\) a finite sequence of scalar functions with common domain \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). Suppose that, at \(x_{0}\in D\), the derivatives \(f_{j}'(x_{0})\) exist for every \(j=1,...,n\). Then, it holds \[ \begin{split} \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) &=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\, \alpha_{j}\,f_{j}'(x_{0}) \\ \left(\prod_{j=1}^{n} \,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) &=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}'(x_{0})\,\left(\prod_{k\neq j}\alpha_{k}\,f_{k}(x_{0})\right). \end{split} \]

Solution:

Fix an accumulation point \(x_0\in D\) and assume that \(f_j'(x_0)\) exists for all \(j=1,\dots,n\). Set \(g_j:=\alpha_j f_j\), so that \(g_j'(x_0)=\alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\).

We will use the two-function rules from Proposition 2

We start with the derivative of the sum of finitely many functions.

For \(h\neq 0\) with \(x_0+h\in D\), we have \[ \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j(x_0+h)-\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j(x_0)}{h} =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j \frac{f_j(x_0+h)-f_j(x_0)}{h}. \] Following Definition 1, we take the limit as \(h\to 0\): \[ \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{j}\,f_{j}\right)'(x_{0}) =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_j(x_0+h)-f_j(x_0)}{h} =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j'(x_0), \] where we passed the limit through the sum because it’s a sum of finitely many elements.

Now, we pass to the derivative of the product of finitely many functions.

Define \[ P_n(x):=\prod_{j=1}^n g_j(x). \]

Base case (\(n=1\)): \(P_1=g_1\), hence \[ P_1'(x_0)=g_1'(x_0) =\sum_{j=1}^1 g_j'(x_0)\prod_{k\neq j} g_k(x_0), \] so the formula holds.

Inductive step: assume that for some \(n\ge 1\), \[ P_n'(x_0)=\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^n g_k(x_0). \] Now \(P_{n+1}=P_n\,g_{n+1}\). By the two-function product rule, \[ P_{n+1}'(x_0)=P_n'(x_0)\,g_{n+1}(x_0)+P_n(x_0)\,g_{n+1}'(x_0). \] Substitute the inductive hypothesis into the first term: \[ \begin{aligned} P_{n+1}'(x_0) &=\left(\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^n g_k(x_0)\right) g_{n+1}(x_0) + \left(\prod_{k=1}^n g_k(x_0)\right) g_{n+1}'(x_0)\\[4pt] &=\sum_{j=1}^n g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^{n+1} g_k(x_0) \;+\; g_{n+1}'(x_0)\prod_{k=1}^n g_k(x_0)\\[4pt] &=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1} g_j'(x_0)\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\neq j}}^{n+1} g_k(x_0). \end{aligned} \] Finally, replacing \(g_j=\alpha_j f_j\) and \(g_j'(x_0)=\alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\), we obtain \[ \left(\prod_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j\right)'(x_0) =\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j f_j'(x_0)\left(\prod_{k\neq j}\alpha_k f_k(x_0)\right). \]

This concludes the proof.

CautionDerivatives of polynomials and rational functions

Exercise 5 Let \(P_{n},Q_{m}\colon \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be polynomial functions. Compute \(P_{n}',Q_{m}'\), and \(R'\), where \(R=\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{m}}\) is defined on the maximal domain \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) on which \(Q_{m}(x)\neq 0\).

Solution:

To compute the derivative of \(P_{n}\), we first write \[ P_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{k}\,x^{k} \] with \(\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\), and then use Exercise 3 together with Exercise 4 to conclude that the derivative \(P_{n}'\) of \(P_{n}\) is the polynomial of order \((n-1)\) given by \[ P_{n}'(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\alpha_{k}\,x^{k-1}. \] A similar result holds for \(Q_{m}'(x)\).

The derivative of \(R(x)=\frac{P_{n}(x)}{Q_{m}(x)}\) can then be immediately obtained using the previous results and Proposition 3:

\[ R'(x)=\frac{P'_{n}(x)Q_{m}(x) - P_{n}(x)Q'_{m}(x)}{(Q_{m}(x))^{2}}. \]

4 Derivative of inverse and composite functions

We previously saw that continuity behaves well under composition of continuous functions and inversion of functions on intervals.

Can we hope for analogous behaviours for differentiable functions? The answer is yes, under suitable hypotheses.

NoteDerivative of composite functions - The chain rule

Proposition 4 Consider the functions \(f\colon D\subseteq \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) and \(g\colon D'\subseteq \mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\), and assume that \(g(D')\subseteq D\). If \(g\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D'\) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(x_{0})\in D\), then the composite function \(r:=f\circ g\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\) and \[ r'(x_{0})=(f\circ g)'(x_{0})=f'(g(x_{0}))\,g'(x_{0}). \]

Proof:

Define the auxiliary function \(\phi\colon D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) by \[ \phi(y):= \left\{ \begin{matrix} \dfrac{f(y)-f(g(x_{0}))}{y-g(x_{0})} & \mbox{ if } y\neq g(x_{0}), \\ & \\ f'(g(x_{0})) & \mbox{ if } y=g(x_{0}) . \end{matrix} \right. \] Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(x_{0})\), it follows from Definition 1 that \[ \lim_{y\rightarrow g(x_{0})}\phi(y)=f'(g(x_{0})), \] so \(\phi\) is continuous at \(g(x_{0})\).

Moreover, Proposition 1 implies that \(g\) is continuous at \(x_{0}\), that is, \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}g(x)=g(x_{0}). \] Since \(\phi\) is continuous at \(g(x_{0})\), limit of composite functions implies that \[ \lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}} \phi(g(x))=\phi(g(x_{0}))=f'(g(x_{0})). \]

For every \(h\neq 0\) such that \(x_{0}+h\in D'\), we have \[ \frac{r(x_{0}+h)-r(x_{0})}{h} =\frac{f(g(x_{0}+h))-f(g(x_{0}))}{h} =\phi(g(x_{0}+h))\,\frac{g(x_{0}+h)-g(x_{0})}{h}. \] Indeed, if \(g(x_{0}+h)\neq g(x_{0})\), the identity follows from the definition of \(\phi\), while if \(g(x_{0}+h)= g(x_{0})\), both sides are equal to \(0\).

Passing to the limit as \(h\to 0\), we obtain \[ \begin{split} r'(x_{0}) &=\lim_{h\to 0}\phi(g(x_{0}+h))\,\frac{g(x_{0}+h)-g(x_{0})}{h} \\ &=\left(\lim_{h\to 0}\phi(g(x_{0}+h))\right)\left(\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(x_{0}+h)-g(x_{0})}{h}\right) \\ &=f'(g(x_{0}))\,g'(x_{0}), \end{split} \] as claimed.
CautionA first exercise on the chain rule

Exercise 6 Compute the derivative of the function \(f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \[ f(x)=\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{3}. \]

Solution:

Define \[ g(x):=x^{2}+1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad h(y):=y^{3}, \] so that \(f=h\circ g\).

From Example 3, Example 1, and Proposition 2, we obtain \[ g'(x)=2x. \] From Exercise 4, we know that \[ h'(y)=3y^{2}. \] Applying Proposition 4, we conclude that \[ f'(x)=h'(g(x))\,g'(x)=3(x^{2}+1)^{2}\cdot 2x=6x(x^{2}+1)^{2}. \]
NoteDerivative of inverse functions

Proposition 5 Let \(I\) be an interval, let \(f\colon I\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be differentiable and invertible on \(I\), and set \(J:=f(I)\). Fix \(x_{0}\in I\) and write \(y_{0}:=f(x_{0})\). If \(f'(x_{0})\neq 0\), then the inverse function \(f^{-1}\colon J\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is differentiable at \(y_{0}\) and \[ \left(f^{-1}\right)'(y_{0})=\frac{1}{f'(x_{0})}. \]

Proof:

Since \(f\) is differentiable on \(I\), Proposition 1 implies that \(f\) is continuous on \(I\). Therefore, continuity of inverse function guarantees that \(f^{-1}\) is continuous on \(J\).

Take \(y\in J\) with \(y\neq y_{0}\), and set \(x:=f^{-1}(y)\). Then \(x\neq x_{0}\) because \(f\) is invertible, and \(y=f(x)\). Hence \[ \frac{f^{-1}(y)-f^{-1}(y_{0})}{y-y_{0}} =\frac{x-x_{0}}{f(x)-f(x_{0})} =\left(\frac{f(x)-f(x_{0})}{x-x_{0}}\right)^{-1}. \]

Now let \(y\rightarrow y_{0}\) in \(J\). Since \(f^{-1}\) is continuous at \(y_{0}\) by continuity of inverse function, we have \[ x=f^{-1}(y)\rightarrow f^{-1}(y_{0})=x_{0}. \] Therefore, \[ \begin{split} \left(f^{-1}\right)'(y_{0}) &=\lim_{y\rightarrow y_{0}}\frac{f^{-1}(y)-f^{-1}(y_{0})}{y-y_{0}} \\ &=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\left(\frac{f(x)-f(x_{0})}{x-x_{0}}\right)^{-1} \\ &=\left(\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\frac{f(x)-f(x_{0})}{x-x_{0}}\right)^{-1} \\ &=\frac{1}{f'(x_{0})}, \end{split} \] where the reciprocal is legitimate because \(f'(x_{0})\neq 0\).
CautionDerivative of root functions

Exercise 7 Let \(n\in \mathbb{N}\) with \(n\geq 1\), and let \(D\) be the maximal domain on which the function \[ p_{n}(x)=x^{n} \] admits an inverse denoted by \(g\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) and given by \(g(y)=y^{\frac{1}{n}}\). Explicitly, \(D=\mathbb{R}\) if \(n\) is odd and \(D=\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty)\) if \(n\) is even.

Use Proposition 5 to compute the derivative of \(g\) at points \(y_{0}\in p_{n}(D)\) for which the proposition applies.

Solution:

Fix \(y_{0}\in p_{n}(D)\) and set \(x_{0}:=g(y_{0})=y_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\). By Exercise 4, the derivative of \(p_{n}\) is \[ p_{n}'(x)=nx^{n-1}. \] Therefore, if \(x_{0}\neq 0\), then \[ p_{n}'(x_{0})=n x_{0}^{n-1}\neq 0, \] and Proposition 5 yields \[ g'(y_{0})=\frac{1}{p_{n}'(x_{0})} =\frac{1}{n x_{0}^{n-1}} =\frac{1}{n\left(y_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{n-1}} =\frac{1}{n}y_{0}^{\frac{1}{n}-1}. \]

If \(n>1\) and \(y_{0}=0\), then \(x_{0}=0\) and \[ p_{n}'(0)=0, \] so Proposition 5 does not apply. Therefore, for \(n>1\), the derivative formula above is valid only at points \(y_{0}\neq 0\).
CautionRight derivative at the origin for even roots

Exercise 8 Let \(\mathbb{N}\ni n\geq 2\) be even. Consider the function \(g\colon \mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) given by \[ g(y)=y^{\frac{1}{n}}. \] Study the right derivative of \(g\) at \(0\).

Solution:

Since \(g(0)=0\), for every \(h>0\) we have \[ \frac{g(0+h)-g(0)}{h} =\frac{h^{\frac{1}{n}}}{h} =h^{\frac{1}{n}-1} =h^{-\frac{n-1}{n}}. \] Because \(-\frac{n-1}{n}<0\), the previous quantity tends to \(+\infty\) as \(h\rightarrow 0^{+}\).

Therefore, \(g\) does not admit a finite right derivative at \(0\). Equivalently, the right difference quotient has unbounded limit \(+\infty\) at the origin.

5 Interpretation of derivative

6 Higher order derivatives, \(C^{k}\)-functions, and smooth functions

It may happen that the derivative of a differentiable function is itself differentiable, and that the same phenomenon can be repeated many times. This leads to the notion of higher order derivatives and, later on, to a useful regularity classification of functions.

NoteHigher order derivatives

Definition 2 Consider the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\).

If the derivative function \(f'\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\in D\), then its derivative at \(x_{0}\) is called the second derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\), and is denoted by \[ f''(x_{0})=(f')'(x_{0}). \] If the derivative of \(f'\) is again differentiable at \(x_{0}\), then we define the third derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\) by \[ f'''(x_{0})=(f'')'(x_{0}), \] and so on.

More generally, define recursively \[ f^{(0)}:=f. \] For \(k\in \mathbb{N}\), if \(f^{(k-1)}\) is differentiable at \(x_{0}\), then the derivative of \(f^{(k-1)}\) at \(x_{0}\) is called the \(k\)-th derivative of \(f\) at \(x_{0}\), and is denoted by \[ f^{(k)}(x_{0})=\left(f^{(k-1)}\right)'(x_{0}). \] We also write \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}f}{\mathrm{d} x^{k}}(x_{0})\equiv f^{(k)}(x_{0}). \]

If \(f^{(k)}(x)\) exists for every \(x\in D\), we say that \(f\) is \(k\) times differentiable in \(D\). As for the first derivative, the domain of \(f^{(k)}\) need not coincide with the domain of \(f\).

Note\(C^{k}\)-functions and smooth functions

Definition 3 Let \(k\in \mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\). We say that the function \(f\colon D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is of class \(C^{k}\) in \(D\) if, for every \(j=0,\dots,k\), the derivative \(f^{(j)}\) exists in \(D\) and is continuous in \(D\). Here, by convention, \(f^{(0)}=f\), so being of class \(C^{0}\) simply means being continuous.

If \(f\) is of class \(C^{k}\) for every \(k\in \mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\), then \(f\) is called a smooth function, or a \(C^{\infty}\)-function.

Clearly, if \(f\) is of class \(C^{k}\) with \(k\geq 1\), then it is also of class \(C^{k-1}\).

CautionHigher derivatives of monomials

Exercise 9 Let \(n\in \mathbb{N}\) and \(\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\). Consider the function \(f_{n}\colon \mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) given by \[ f_{n}(x)=\alpha_{n}x^{n}. \] Compute its \(k\)-th derivative for every \(k\in\mathbb{N}\).

Solution:

From Exercise 4, we know that \[ f_{n}'(x)=n\alpha_{n}x^{n-1}. \] Applying the same result repeatedly, we obtain \[ \begin{split} f_{n}''(x)&=n(n-1)\alpha_{n}x^{n-2}, \\ f_{n}'''(x)&=n(n-1)(n-2)\alpha_{n}x^{n-3}, \end{split} \] and, in general, \[ f_{n}^{(k)}(x)=n(n-1)\cdots (n-k+1)\,\alpha_{n}\,x^{n-k} =\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\,\alpha_{n}\,x^{n-k} \] for every \(k=1,\dots,n\).

For \(k=n\), this gives \[ f_{n}^{(n)}(x)=n!\,\alpha_{n}, \] which is a constant function. Therefore, by Example 1, \[ f_{n}^{(n+1)}(x)=0. \] Applying again Example 1, we conclude that \[ f_{n}^{(k)}(x)=0 \] for every \(k\geq n+1\).
NotePolynomials are smooth

Proposition 6 Every polynomial function \(P\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is smooth.

Proof:

Write \[ P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}x^{j}. \] Fix \(k\in\mathbb{N}\). By Exercise 9, for every \(j=0,\dots,n\), the \(k\)-th derivative of the monomial \(x\mapsto \alpha_{j}x^{j}\) exists on \(\mathbb{R}\) and is either a monomial or the zero function. In particular, it is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Using Exercise 3 repeatedly, we conclude that \(P^{(k)}\) exists on \(\mathbb{R}\) and is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\). Since \(k\) is arbitrary, \(P\) is of class \(C^{k}\) for every \(k\in\mathbb{N}\), hence it is smooth.
